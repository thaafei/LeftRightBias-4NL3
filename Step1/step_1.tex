\section{Annotation Instructions}
For our task of identifying the primary category of a YouTube video, we've established some guidelines to help annotators with our classification task. To start, a quick summary of the task itself.
Our group have determined 12 categories of YouTube videos, sourced from YouTube's category list \href{https://mixedanalytics.com/blog/list-of-youtube-video-category-ids/}{(see here)} and our team's own assumptions. 
\\ \\
The task is to answer the classification question, \textbf{"What is the main category of this YouTube video?"}. You can achieve this by reading both the \textbf{title} and \textbf{description} in order to determine the most probably category. In other words, each sample should be labeled with only 1 of the 12 categories.
\\
Use the guideline below to support your decision, but ultimately your best judgement as there will be edge cases.
Overall guidelines for annotation are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Use both the video title and the description
  \item Do NOT google the YouTube video, as the whole point is to determine based on just the title and description, which watching the video will defeat. However, you may google definition of words.
  \item If a video title and description don't align, place more emphasize on the context mentioned from the title.
  \item Identify the primary intent, think "What is the video mainly trying to do?". For example, a cooking vlog in Paris should be categorized as Food \& Cooking instead of Lifestyle. Since the primary content is on the cooking itself and the creator just happened to be in Paris.
  \item Do not categorize based on the channel, we should assume all videos are independent and not categorize all videos similarly if it came from the same channel.
  \item If a video can be grouped in multiple categories, choose the category that is more specific. Try thinking: "If I'm on YouTube and browsing by category, which category would the video most likely be grouped under?"
\end{enumerate}
\noindent Guidelines for each category are listed below, note that they are not definitive.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Food \& Cooking}: Recipe, Cooking Technique
  \item \textbf{Video Games}: Gameplay, Game Reviews, Streaming Highlights
  \item \textbf{Sports \& Games}: Sport Games, Commentary, Board Games, Training Specific to Sport
  \item \textbf{Music}: Music Videos, Karaoke Videos
  \item \textbf{Education}: School Coursework, Math, General Knowledge Videos, Documentary
  \item \textbf{Shopping}: Product Reviews, Shopping Hauls
  \item \textbf{Vehicles}: Car Shows, Racing
  \item \textbf{Lifestyle}: Traveling, Day-in-the-life, Vlogs
  \item \textbf{News}: Politics, Breaking News, Current Events
  \item \textbf{Health \& Fitness}: Gym, Workout Plans, Diet
  \item \textbf{Business}: Investing, Economy, Entrepreneurship, Finance
  \item \textbf{Digital Media}: Movies, Animated Shorts, Fan Fiction, Memes
\end{enumerate}

\section{Dataset}
The dataset we are using is called \href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/AdamLucek/youtube-titles}{youtube-titles} by Adam Lucek from Hugging face. It contains 4.9k videos across 50 YouTube Channels sourced from 2024. The dataset is licensed under the MIT License.
The following columns can be found in the dataset:
\begin{itemize}
  \item channel\_name: The name of the channel
  \item channel\_url: The URL of the channel
  \item channel\_id: The unique identifier of the channel
  \item \textbf{video\_title}: The title of the video
  \item video\_id: The unique identifier of the video
  \item \textbf{video\_description}: First 500 characters of the video Description
  \item prompt: Plausible prompt for generating this title (for LLM training)
  \item gemma2\_9b\_it\_format: Formatted prompt and title combo for gemma 2 9b instruction tuned model fine tuning
\end{itemize}
We will only use the \texttt{video\_title} and \texttt{video\_description} columns, as bolded in the list above.
The dataset is unlabeled for our classification purposes and will be stored as a csv file called \texttt{dataset.csv} in our GitHub repository, \href{https://github.com/thaafei/YouTubeCategories-4NL3}{YoutubeCategories-4NL3}, where each row represents one YouTube video.

Below are several edge cases and dataset characteristics we found interesting during annotation, because they create ambiguity even with detailed category guidelines.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sponsor-heavy descriptions that contain little to no topical signal.} \\
  Example: \\
  \textbf{Title:} \emph{I made him cry..}\\
  The corresponding description is dominated by sponsor/affiliate content (e.g., discount codes and links), with minimal information about what happens in the video. This is interesting because our rules require using both title and description, but in practice the description can be uninformative noise. When the title is also vague, the instance becomes difficult to label without channel context (which we explicitly disallow). This suggests models may need robustness to “ad-only” or low-signal descriptions.

  \item \textbf{Mixed-intent videos that sit between multiple categories (boundary ambiguity).}\\
  Example: \\
  \textbf{Title:} \emph{Eating McDonald's Happy Meals until I get EVERY POKEMON CARD!!}\\
  This single instance plausibly overlaps multiple categories: it involves \emph{food/eating}, a \emph{collecting challenge} (which can resemble Lifestyle/entertainment), and \emph{Pok\'emon} (which can cue Video Games or Digital Media depending on interpretation). This is interesting because our category list does not include a generic “Entertainment/Challenges” label, so annotators must map these videos to the \emph{closest primary intent} category. These boundary cases are likely to be a major source of annotator disagreement.

  \item \textbf{Non-English titles and mixed-script text that can still strongly signal a category.}\\
  Example: \\

  \item \textbf{Systematic text noise in scraped metadata (URLs, mojibake, and symbols) that affects preprocessing.}
  Beyond individual multilingual examples, we observed that many descriptions include heavy URL blocks, affiliate text, and occasional mojibake/encoding artifacts (e.g., corrupted arrows or emoji sequences). This is interesting because it can reduce the effective semantic signal available for classification and can distort tokenization, causing models to overweight frequent non-content tokens (URLs, codes, repeated symbols). This suggests we may want to normalize Unicode, standardize or remove URLs, and apply consistent truncation or filtering rules for descriptions in later experiments.
